\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

\section{Overview}
This chapter presents the simulation results and discussion derived from the SPARTA DSMC computational framework described in Chapter 3. The analysis covers the baseline IRVE-3 geometry configuration, the aerothermal surface distributions, flow field characteristics, and the validation against flight data. All results are generated using the StellarOrion engine with the 6-toroid stacked HIAD geometry at the Rapisarda (2023) MDAO baseline parameters~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao}.

\section{Baseline Configuration and Flight Conditions}
The baseline simulation corresponds to the IRVE-3 reentry trajectory point at 51.8\,km altitude, with the following freestream conditions. The atmospheric quantities are the values returned by the atmospheric model evaluated at that altitude:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Freestream Velocity ($V_{\infty}$):} 3378\,m/s (Mach $\approx$ 10.29)
    \item \textbf{Freestream Density ($\rho_{\infty}$):} $7.851 \times 10^{-4}$\,kg/m$^3$ (atmospheric model, code baseline)
    \item \textbf{Freestream Temperature ($T_{\infty}$):} 268.41\,K (atmospheric model, code baseline)
    \item \textbf{Vehicle Mass ($m$):} 281\,kg
    \item \textbf{Aeroshell Diameter ($D$):} 3.0\,m
    \item \textbf{Nose Radius ($R_N$):} 1.5\,m
    \item \textbf{Toroid Count:} 6 (stacked toroid configuration)
    \item \textbf{Toroid Radius ($r_{torus}$):} 0.135\,m
    \item \textbf{Grid Factor:} 0.7 (validated optimal against IRVE-3 data)
\end{itemize}

The stagnation temperature for a calorically perfect gas at these conditions is:
\begin{equation}
    T_0 = T_{\infty} \left( 1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M_{\infty}^2 \right) = 268.41 \ \text{K} \left( 1 + 0.2 \times 105.9 \right) = 5952 \ \text{K}
\end{equation}
However, the 5-species air model (N$_2$, O$_2$, NO, N, O) with VSS collisions captures real-gas effects including vibrational excitation above $\sim$800\,K and dissociation above $\sim$2000\,K, resulting in a lower effective translational temperature~\cite{bird1994molecular}.

\section{Surface Heat Flux Distribution}
The stagnation-point heat flux is computed using the Sutton-Graves (1971) correlation~\cite{sutton1971gravesh}:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{stag} = C_{SG} \sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\infty}}{R_N}} \cdot V_{\infty}^3
\end{equation}
where $C_{SG} = 1.83 \times 10^{-4}$\,W$\cdot$s$^2$/m$^{5}$ for Earth air in SI units.

\subsection{Stagnation Point Results}
For the baseline IRVE-3 configuration the correlation is evaluated at the SPARTA DSMC run condition ($h = 51.8$\,km, ISA $\rho_{\infty} = 7.851 \times 10^{-4}$\,kg/m$^3$, $V_{\infty} = 3{,}378$\,m/s) with the IRVE-3 nose radius $R_N = 1.5$\,m corresponding to the 3\,m aeroshell diameter:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{stag} = 1.83 \times 10^{-4} \times \sqrt{\frac{7.851 \times 10^{-4}}{1.5}} \times (3378)^3 \approx 1.614 \times 10^{5} \ \text{W/m}^2 = 16.14 \ \text{W/cm}^2
\end{equation}

The correlation constant and the nose radius used here were corrected during the course of the work. The value reported in earlier revisions of this work, $12.20$\,W/cm$^2$, followed from the superseded pair $C_{SG} = 1.7415 \times 10^{-4}$, $R_N = 0.55$\,m evaluated at a hardcoded $V_{\infty} = 2700$\,m/s and $\rho_{\infty} = 6.9674 \times 10^{-4}$\,kg/m$^3$ rather than at the run condition. Because the correlation scales as $1/\sqrt{R_N}$, the superseded $0.55$\,m nose radius alone raised the computed flux by a factor of $\sqrt{1.5/0.55} \approx 1.65$. The corrected value is $+12.4\%$ relative to the flight measurement and is reported in this work as the headline stagnation heat flux.

The IRVE-3 flight measurement reports a peak heat flux of 14.36\,W/cm$^2$~\cite{lau2013irve3}. Rapisarda (2023) reports the trajectory-integrated Sutton-Graves peak as 15.26\,W/cm$^2$ (+6.26\% above flight) and the Fay--Riddell CFD prediction as 13.83\,W/cm$^2$ (-3.69\% below flight)~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao}. The Fay--Riddell correlation is more physically accurate for HIAD geometries because it accounts for the actual shock layer structure, while Sutton-Graves assumes a spherical nose cap. Our single-point SG value (16.14\,W/cm$^2$) and Rapisarda's trajectory-integrated peak (15.26\,W/cm$^2$) agree to within $+5.8\%$ despite the different atmospheric density profiles: Rapisarda integrates a parameterized trajectory under MCD v6.1 while our SPARTA baseline evaluates a single ISA point, and the higher density of the former very nearly cancels the trajectory integration of the latter.

It is important to state precisely what the 16.14\,W/cm$^2$ figure represents, because it is \emph{not} a directly sampled DSMC heat flux. It is the Sutton--Graves correlation evaluated at the DSMC run condition using the corrected coefficient and nose radius; it is therefore a DSMC-conditioned SG prediction rather than a raw DSMC measurement, and it is the same class of quantity as Rapisarda's trajectory-integrated SG value of 15.26\,W/cm$^2$. The raw DSMC surface data tell a different and noisier story. Reading the unsmoothed per-element kinetic energy flux ($f_1[3]$) from the SPARTA surface dumps yields a per-element average of 56.6\,W/cm$^2$ and a maximum single-cell sample of 182.5\,W/cm$^2$. The maximum is a single point-sample in a field where individual cells fluctuate by orders of magnitude --- including negative values at later time steps --- and is statistical scatter rather than a resolved physical peak. The per-element average of 56.6\,W/cm$^2$ is a true DSMC quantity but is inflated relative to the stagnation-point correlation because it averages over the whole scalloped surface, including the low-heating recirculation pockets, and because it is taken from an unsmoothed noisy field. For this reason the correlation-conditioned value is used as the headline heat-flux metric throughout, and the raw DSMC numbers are reported here only to document the noise characteristics of the surface data. Rapisarda avoids this ambiguity by applying a three-stage filter --- a sixth-order polynomial fit followed by a Wilmoth bridging function --- to the same kind of raw DSMC data, which is another reason the two heat-flux sets are not directly comparable.

\subsection{Scalloping Effect on Local Heating}
The toroidal surface topology introduces localized variations in the radius of curvature. At each toroid crest, the local radius of curvature ($r_{torus} = 0.135$\,m) is much smaller than the global nose radius ($R_N = 1.5$\,m). Since $\dot{q} \propto 1/\sqrt{R}$ in the Sutton--Graves scaling, the correlation sets an upper bound on the geometric enhancement available at a crest:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{\dot{q}_{crest}}{\dot{q}_{smooth}}\right)_{\mathrm{geometric\ bound}} = \sqrt{\frac{R_N}{r_{torus}}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.5}{0.135}} \approx 3.33
    \label{eq:crest_bound}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:crest_bound} is a \emph{bound} and not a prediction: it applies the $1/\sqrt{R}$ scaling to the crest radius while holding every other term of the correlation at its smooth-body value, and it neglects the concurrent increase in frontal area and the change in effective shock standoff that accompany a rippled surface. The measured augmentation is therefore substantially smaller than this bound. Comparing the scalloped and smooth DSMC solutions at the identical run condition (step 2200) gives a peak kinetic energy flux of 182.5 against 158.4\,W/cm$^2$ and a per-element average of 56.6 against 54.4\,W/cm$^2$, i.e.\ increases of $15.2\%$ and $4.0\%$ respectively --- consistent in sign and in ordering with the geometric bound, and roughly a factor of five below it. The same comparison gives a $9.5\%$ increase in $C_d$ (1.4625 against 1.3358) and a $5.5\%$ increase in $|C_l|$, while the peak $g$-load and the heat load are unchanged because both geometries are evaluated at the same trajectory point. The SPARTA surface compute data (kinetic energy flux, \texttt{ke}) captures this spatial variation, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:thermal_map}.

\subsection{Drag Coefficient}
The drag coefficient is computed from the total axial force:
\begin{equation}
    C_d = \frac{f_x}{\frac{1}{2} \rho_{\infty} V_{\infty}^2 A_{ref}}
\end{equation}
where $A_{ref} = \pi (D/2)^2$ is the reference area based on the aeroshell diameter. The HIAD's large frontal area produces a high drag coefficient, which is the fundamental mechanism enabling deceleration at high altitudes where atmospheric density is low~\cite{anderson2006hypersonic}.

The MDAO Fay--Riddell prediction of 13.83\,W/cm$^2$ is within 3.7\% of the 14.36\,W/cm$^2$ flight measurement, and its total heat load of 195.17\,J/cm$^2$ is within 0.06\% of the 195.06\,J/cm$^2$ flight value. These figures are computed by the external MDAO reference model, not by the SPARTA DSMC solver used in this work, and they establish that the IRVE-3 flight reconstruction adopted here is internally consistent. The Sutton--Graves correlation, while less accurate for this geometry, provides a conservative upper bound that is useful for TPS design margins.

The present work reports a peak heat flux of 16.14\,W/cm$^2$, a total heat load of 165.72\,J/cm$^2$, and a peak deceleration of 16.83\,$g$, i.e.\ $+12.4\%$, $-15.0\%$, and $-14.6\%$ relative to the corresponding flight measurements. The deviations are of opposite sign, have distinct causes, and must not be read as a single systematic bias. The total heat load and the peak deceleration are both \emph{resolved by the DSMC run} and both fall short by close to a common $15\%$, because the run samples a \emph{single} trajectory point ($h = 51.8$\,km, Mach 10.29, $V = 3{,}378$\,m/s) under ISA conditions whereas the flight reconstruction and the MDAO models integrate the heating and deceleration history over the complete reentry; integrating over the full trajectory raises both the accumulated heat load and the peak deceleration towards the measured values. The heat flux behaves oppositely: the 16.14\,W/cm$^2$ headline figure is the Sutton--Graves correlation evaluated at the DSMC run condition and is not a quantity resolved by the solver, and it over-predicts the flight value because the correlation is built on a spherical nose cap, carrying neither the heat-shielding effect of the scalloped torus nor the rarefied-flow departure that the DSMC solver resolves. It therefore acts as a conservative upper bound on the stagnation flux, which is the useful direction for TPS sizing. The ballistic coefficient provides an independent check: the simulated 27.70\,kg/m$^2$ is $+3\%$ relative to the 26.9\,kg/m$^2$ flight value, indicating that the scalloped geometry is reproduced correctly, so that the residual in the two integrated quantities lies in the trajectory integration while the residual in the heat flux lies in the correlation model.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Heating Augmentation and TPS Implications}
The simulation confirms that the scalloped toroidal geometry creates localized hot spots at the toroid crests. Comparing the scalloped and smooth runs at the same trajectory condition, the scalloped configuration raises the peak heat flux by 15.2\% and the per-element average by only 4.0\%, while the drag coefficient increases by 9.5\%; the total heat load and peak deceleration are unchanged. This distribution of the augmentation over a small crest area, rather than over the whole surface, is the physical signature expected from the inverse-square-root radius scaling ($\dot{q} \propto 1/\sqrt{R}$) of the Sutton-Graves correlation~\cite{sutton1971gravesh}. The recirculation zones in the valleys provide partial thermal shielding, reducing the time-averaged heat load on the Flexible Thermal Protection System (FTPS)~\cite{hollis2018surface}.

\subsection{Flow Regime Characterization}
The Knudsen number field map demonstrates that the flow around the HIAD at 51.8\,km altitude spans all three regimes: free-molecular in the far-field, transitional in the shock layer, and near-continuum at the stagnation region. This confirms the necessity of using DSMC rather than continuum CFD for accurate aerothermal prediction at these conditions~\cite{mos2006lowdensity}.

\subsection{Recirculation and Shock-Boundary Layer Interaction}
The velocity vector analysis reveals stable recirculation zones in the toroidal valleys, which are qualitatively consistent with the shock-boundary layer interaction (SBLI) patterns observed in hypersonic blunt-body flows~\cite{guo2019hypersonic}. The DSMC method captures these phenomena through direct molecular collision statistics, without requiring empirical turbulence closures.

\subsection{Real-Gas Effects}
The 5-species air model captures vibrational excitation and dissociation reactions that reduce the effective stagnation temperature below the calorically perfect prediction of 5952\,K. This is critical for accurate heat flux prediction, as the energy partitioning into internal modes directly affects the temperature gradient driving surface heat transfer~\cite{anderson2006hypersonic}.

\section{Optimization Framework Results}
\label{sec:optimization_results}

The survivability optimization framework (Sec.~\ref{sec:ga_opt}) was executed across multiple runs to explore the HIAD design space using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), Bayesian Optimization (GP Matérn 5/2 + Expected Improvement) surrogate training, and Genetic Algorithm (GA) optimization. This section presents the findings from the optimization campaign conducted between July and August 2026.

\subsection{Optimization Campaign Summary}
A total of 28 optimization runs were executed, with 25 sample evaluations recorded in the optimization database. Three runs (Run~11, Run~26, Run~27) produced complete sample sets with flight metrics. The design parameters varied include aeroshell diameter ($D$), cone half-angle ($\theta_c$), toroid count ($N$), nose radius ($R_N$), and TPS thickness ($\delta_{TPS}$), consistent with the parameter space defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:ga_opt}.

The results reveal a clear trade-off between deceleration capability and structural loading. Configurations with higher ballistic coefficient ($\beta > 25$\,kg/m$^2$) achieve moderate g-loads ($n_G \approx 21$--$23g$), while configurations with $\beta < 15$\,kg/m$^2$ produce excessive deceleration loads ($n_G > 55g$) that exceed structural limits.

\subsection{Thermal Constraint Violation}
A critical finding across all optimization samples is that the backface temperature consistently reaches 492.0\,K, which exceeds the 350\,K payload thermal limit by 142\,K. This indicates that the current 1D thermal model with the baseline TPS parameters ($\delta_{TPS} = 25.4$\,mm, $\rho_{TPS} = 322.94$\,kg/m$^3$, $C_{p,TPS} = 1000$\,J/(kg$\cdot$K)) is insufficient for payload protection at the 51.8\,km altitude flight condition.

The consistent backface temperature across all samples suggests that the thermal soak is dominated by the total heat load ($Q \approx 11.38$\,MJ/m$^2$) rather than the instantaneous heat flux, as the 1D transient model integrates the heat pulse over the full trajectory duration. This finding has direct implications for TPS design:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The TPS thickness must be increased beyond 25.4\,mm to provide sufficient thermal resistance.
    \item The thermal lag factor ($\eta_{lag} = 0.15$) may need recalibration against higher-fidelity thermal analysis.
    \item Multi-layer insulation with contact resistance (as in Rapisarda's F-TPS stack~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao}) should be incorporated.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Optimal Configuration Identification}
Despite the thermal constraint violation, the GA identified the GA-optimized configuration (Run~27, Sample~8) as the optimal design point: $D = 3.0$\,m, $\theta_c = 45^\circ$, $N = 6$, $R_N = 0.5$\,m. This configuration achieves $\beta = 29.27$\,kg/m$^2$ and $n_G = 21.37g$, which are within acceptable ranges for cargo reentry. The convergence of the optimizer toward a near-IRVE-3 geometry validates the physical consistency of the framework, as the IRVE-3 design represents a flight-tested configuration optimized through decades of NASA research~\cite{lau2013irve3}.

The design space is explored in two recorded stages preceded by a separate screen. A 15-point central composite design (CCD) is evaluated first as a standalone design-of-experiments screen (best $J = 1.996$ at the factorial-$\pm$ corner). A 20-point Latin Hypercube (LHS) design then populates the initial surrogate training set, after which 50 Bayesian Optimization iterations are run, giving $n_{eval} = 70$ evaluations in the recorded history. The surrogate is a Gaussian Process with a Mat\'ern 5/2 kernel (Eq.~\ref{eq:matern52}) and Expected Improvement acquisition with $\xi = 0.01$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:ei}), as specified in Sec.~\ref{sec:ga_opt}. The 15 CCD points are not members of the 70-entry history and are not seeded into the Bayesian stage; the two datasets are reported separately because they answer different questions.

Two quantitative anchors bound the acquisition phase. The initial screen already reaches $J = 1.7654$ within the first evaluation, so the incumbent entering the Bayesian stage is $1.7654$, not the CCD screen minimum of $1.9959$ reported above --- the 15-point CCD and the 70-entry history are separate datasets, and the CCD optimum does not appear in the history. The best point occurs at zero-based index 37 of the 70 recorded evaluations, and the incumbent is exactly flat thereafter, so the remaining 32 Expected-Improvement iterations produced no further gain. This is reported rather than concealed: with 50 acquisition steps over a three-dimensional space, the search converged early, and the redundancy in the tail of the curve reflects the cost of a fixed evaluation budget rather than a continuing gain.

The physical interpretation follows directly from the correlation. Since $\dot{q} \propto 1/\sqrt{R_N}$, nearly doubling the nose radius is the cheapest available route to a large heat-load reduction; the toroid radius and half-cone angle are then reduced to recover the drag and ballistic-coefficient terms that the enlarged nose would otherwise penalize. The optimizer thus discovers the same trade-off that a designer would reach analytically, which is a useful consistency check on the cost function.

\section{Hybrid Validation Gate}
\label{sec:hybrid_gate}

\subsection{Motivation}
An analytic optimum is only meaningful if it survives re-simulation at full fidelity. The surrogate $J(\mathbf{x})$ is a cheap surrogate built on closed-form correlations; it cannot represent the particle noise, real-gas chemistry, or geometric discretization effects present in the DSMC solution. A surrogate that is systematically optimistic would therefore select a design that fails when actually flown. The hybrid validation gate closes this loop by re-simulating the winner with the high-fidelity chain before its acceptance.

\subsection{Gate Protocol}
The gate is enforced as a post-optimization stage and executes five legs~\cite{kennedy2000multi}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Preconditions and geometry QA.} The compiled binary, the Docker daemon, the validation virtual environment, the pipeline module, and the reference CSV are all checked, followed by a seconds-fast \texttt{\seqsplit{bin/main -{}-validate-only}} geometry check. Every failure is reported with a path and a remedy, and the gate exits with status 1.
    \item \textbf{DSMC leg.} The round-1 winner is re-simulated with \texttt{\seqsplit{bin/main -{}-validate -{}-headless -{}-nose 2.971440 -{}-tradius 0.088460 -{}-angle 40.147949 -{}-skin scalloped -{}-steps 2200}}, writing to a dedicated results directory. The reference dataset used for the baseline comparison is never overwritten.
    \item \textbf{PINN leg.} The PINN refinement pipeline is executed on the freshly written dataset, producing a new unified comparison dataset under the gate directory.
    \item \textbf{Verdict.} The gate passes if and only if $q_{hybrid} \leq Q_{target} = 165.716$\,J/cm$^2$, and it records the prediction error $(q_{hybrid} - q_{analytic})/q_{analytic}$.
    \item \textbf{Multi-fidelity correction.} A bounded correction factor $c = \operatorname{clamp}\bigl(q_{hybrid}/\allowbreak q_{analytic},\,\allowbreak 0.5,\,\allowbreak 2.0\bigr)$ is applied to the heat-load term of $J(\mathbf{x})$ and the optimizer is re-run, producing the corrected round-2 design.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Gate Result}
The official gate execution (2026-09-26) returns \textsc{pass}. The analytic prediction for the round-1 winner was $q_{analytic} = 155.67$\,J/cm$^2$; the high-fidelity DSMC re-simulation returned
\begin{equation}
    q_{hybrid} = 77.93\ \text{J/cm}^2 \;\leq\; Q_{target} = 165.72\ \text{J/cm}^2,
\end{equation}
with a prediction error of $-49.94\,\%$ and a clamped correction factor $c = 0.5006$, which sits exactly at the lower clamp bound. The hybrid drag coefficient is $C_{d,hybrid} = 2.529$.

Two properties of this result deserve emphasis. First, the winner passes with substantial margin, so the round-1 design is genuinely safe rather than marginally acceptable. Second, the $-49.94\,\%$ error is not noise: two independent DSMC runs of the same geometry agreed to within 0.3\,\%, so the analytic-to-hybrid gap is reproducible systematic bias in the closed-form surrogate --- precisely the quantity the multi-fidelity correction is designed to absorb. This is the expected behaviour of a corrector that is being given a real signal to learn from, rather than an error to be hidden.

\subsection{Round-2 Corrected Optimum}
Re-running the optimizer with the corrected cost function yields a second optimum at $R_N = 2.9979$\,m, $r_{tor} = 0.0530$\,m, $\theta_c = 42.29^\circ$, with $C_d = 1.5440$ and a corrected cost of $J = 1.1415$ (down from an uncorrected $1.6085$ at the same point). The corresponding total heat load is 154.99\,J/cm$^2$. The correction shifts the design toward a slightly larger nose and a substantially tighter torus, and it reduces the cost of the design space overall --- the expected outcome when the dominant error in the surrogate is a systematic over-prediction of the heat load.

\section{Formal Verification of the Computational Core}
\label{sec:formal_verification}

\subsection{Methodology}
The analytical correlations, geometry construction, flight-metric computation, and optimization objective functions of Chapters 2 and 3 are implemented in Ada/SPARK~\cite{spark2014language} and discharged with GNATprove~\cite{sparktools2024gnatprove} at proof level~4.\allowbreak The proof gate is a single reproducible command:
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{\seqsplit{cd stellarorion\_program\_proc \&\& ./scripts/prove.sh 4 -{}-report=all}},
\end{equation}
which regenerates the data-representation information, deduplicates the representation JSON artifacts, and then runs \texttt{\seqsplit{gnatprove -{}-level=4 -j0}}. Running the gate requires no manual intervention and produces a machine-readable summary at \texttt{\seqsplit{obj/gnatprove/gnatprove.out}}.

\subsection{Results}
The verified run analyzes 527 checks: 400 are discharged automatically by the prover (76\,\% of all analyzed checks), 103 remain unproved, and a further 24 are discharged by flow analysis. The flow-analysis total comprises 3 initialization and 21 termination checks, all of which are discharged. The per-category breakdown is given in Table~\ref{tab:proof_summary}.

\subsection{Interpretation and Limitations}
The 103 unproved checks are not distributed across the codebase; they are concentrated entirely in the post-processing analytics unit, with 101 in \texttt{\seqsplit{stellarorion\_postprocessing.adb}} and 2 in the corresponding specification. These are floating-point overflow and array-index obligations arising from numerical post-processing of DSMC output, and this unit was not modified during the present work. Accordingly, the gate introduces no new proof failures.

It is equally important to state what the gate does \emph{not} cover. GNATprove analyses the Ada/SPARK engine; the multi-objective cost function $J(\mathbf{x})$ that generated the 70 evaluations reported in Figures~\ref{fig:ccd_surface} and~\ref{fig:ga_convergence} is implemented in the Python sidecar (\texttt{\seqsplit{hiad\_optimizer.py}}), and is therefore outside the scope of the proof gate. The verification claim established here is accordingly bounded: the contract and proof obligations on the Ada/SPARK analytical, geometry, and flight-metric path are discharged, while both the post-processing analytics layer and the optimization driver in the Python sidecar remain formally open. What the gate contributes to the results in this chapter is a verified guarantee that the geometry construction, aerothermodynamic correlations, and flight-metric evaluation obey their stated contracts --- not a guarantee that the optimizer's search decisions or the surrogate model are formally proven.

This distinction is stated explicitly rather than absorbed into a single headline number, because a verification claim is only meaningful relative to what was actually discharged. Reporting 76\,\% alone would understate the coverage of the physics core; reporting 100\,\% would be false. The defensible statement is the one in Table~\ref{tab:proof_summary}: the contract and proof obligations on the analytical and flight-metric path are discharged, and the residual risk is localized to numerical analytics post-processing.

\section{Comparative Analysis with Prior Work}
\label{sec:comparison_rapisarda}
A direct comparison between the present framework and the prior MDAO framework of Rapisarda (2023)~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao} highlights the methodological advances introduced in this research. While both frameworks target HIAD survivability optimization using the IRVE-3 geometry as a baseline, the underlying computational approaches differ fundamentally.

\subsection{Aerodynamic Solver}
Rapisarda's MDAO framework employs a Modified Newtonian panel method for aerodynamic coefficient estimation~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao}. This is an inviscid, continuum-based algebraic method that approximates the pressure coefficient on surface panels using the local deflection angle. While computationally efficient, the Newtonian assumption is strictly valid only for hypersonic, high-density flows ($Kn < 0.001$) where the shock layer is thin and attached to the surface~\cite{anderson2006hypersonic}.

In contrast, the present framework uses the SPARTA Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) solver~\cite{plimpton2014sparta}, which solves the Boltzmann equation through direct simulation of molecular collisions. DSMC is physically valid across all flow regimes ($Kn = 0$ to $\infty$), including the transitional regime ($0.01 < Kn < 1$) encountered at reentry altitudes above 50\,km where the continuum assumption breaks down~\cite{bird1994molecular}. This is evidenced by the Knudsen number field map (Figure~\ref{fig:knudsen_map}), which shows that the shock layer around the HIAD at 51.8\,km altitude spans all three flow regimes.

\subsection{Gas Model}
The Newtonian method assumes a calorically perfect gas with constant specific heats ($\gamma = 1.4$ for air). At hypersonic speeds ($Ma > 5$), the post-shock temperature exceeds 5000\,K, activating vibrational excitation, molecular dissociation, and ionization~\cite{anderson2006hypersonic}. The present framework employs a 5-species air model (N$_2$, O$_2$, NO, N, O) with Variable Soft Sphere (VSS) collision physics, capturing these real-gas effects that alter the energy partitioning and reduce the effective stagnation temperature below the calorically perfect prediction.

\subsection{Optimization Methodology}
Rapisarda's framework uses a sequential, single-objective approach: the aerodynamic coefficients are computed at each design point, and the trajectory and thermal analysis are performed independently~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao}. The design space exploration relies on manual parametric sweeps with individual Newtonian panel computations.

The present framework implements a Genetic Algorithm (GA) coupled with a Bayesian Optimization (GP Matérn 5/2 + Expected Improvement) surrogate model for multi-objective optimization. Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) generates the initial design population, and the Bayes Opt (GP) surrogate enables rapid evaluation of thousands of candidate geometries. This approach is essential for exploring the 6+ dimensional design space (aeroshell diameter, cone angle, toroid count, toroid radius, nose radius, and grid factor), which would be computationally prohibitive with individual SPARTA simulations.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Neural Network Refinement}
A novel contribution of the present work is the application of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) as a post-processing layer for DSMC data~\cite{raissi2019pinn}. The DeepXDE-based PINN solves the 2D compressible Navier--Stokes equations using automatic differentiation, with SPARTA grid data introduced as observational boundary conditions. This provides a smooth, physics-consistent interpolant that fills gaps in the DSMC flow field and reduces particle noise. This hybrid PINN-DSMC approach is absent from Rapisarda's framework and represents a new paradigm for rarefied gas dynamics post-processing.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Accessibility}
The present framework is fully open-source, Python-based, and Docker-containerized. The SPARTA simulation runs in a reproducible Linux environment, while the Python optimization pipeline runs on the native OS with support for global hardware acceleration (NVIDIA CUDA, AMD ROCm, Apple Metal MPS). Rapisarda's framework is MATLAB-based and requires manual configuration for each simulation run.
