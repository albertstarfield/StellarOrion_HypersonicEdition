\subsection{Derivation Chain}
The methodology is constructed by chaining the theory anchors through a sequence of mathematical derivations. Each step introduces a specific equation or method that is implemented in the software pipeline.

\paragraph{Step 1: Boltzmann Equation (TA1, TA2, TA4).}
The Boltzmann transport equation governs the evolution of the molecular velocity distribution function $f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}, t)$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f + \frac{\mathbf{F}}{m} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{v}} f = \left(\frac{\delta f}{\delta t}\right)_{\text{coll}}
\label{eq:boltzmann_dsmc}
\end{equation}
where the right-hand side is the collision integral, evaluated under the assumption of molecular chaos (anchor TA2). The equilibrium solution is the Maxwell--Boltzmann distribution (anchor TA4).

\paragraph{Step 2: DSMC Method (TA6).}
Bird's Direct Simulation Monte Carlo method~\cite{bird1994molecular} provides a stochastic solution to Equation~\eqref{eq:boltzmann_dsmc}. As the number of simulated particles $N \to \infty$, the DSMC solution converges to the exact Boltzmann solution (anchor TA6). The DSMC algorithm proceeds in three steps per time step:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Advection:} Particles are moved ballistically for $\Delta t$.
    \item \textbf{Collisions:} Pairs are selected within each cell using the VSS collision model.
    \item \textbf{Sampling:} Macroscopic properties (density, temperature, velocity) are accumulated in each cell.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Step 3: Navier--Stokes Limit (A3).}
In the continuum limit ($Kn \to 0$), the Boltzmann equation reduces to the compressible Navier--Stokes equations via the Chapman--Enskog expansion~\cite{chapman1970kinetic}. 

\paragraph{Step 4: Sutton--Graves Correlation (A5).}
For rapid engineering estimates, the stagnation-point heat flux is approximated by~\cite{sutton1971gravesh}:
\begin{equation}
\dot{q}_s = k \sqrt{\frac{\rho_\infty}{R_n}} \, V_\infty^3
\label{eq:sutton_graves_methodology}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is a gas-specific constant, $\rho_\infty$ is the freestream density, $R_n$ is the nose radius, and $V_\infty$ is the flight velocity. This correlation is used both for initial thermal loading estimates and as a validation benchmark against DSMC results.

\paragraph{Step 5: Genetic-Algorithm Optimization.}
The closed-form cost of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ga_cost} is minimized by a simple genetic algorithm: a population of fifty designs is evolved with tournament selection, uniform crossover, Gaussian mutation and elitism until the best cost stagnates. Because the cost is analytic, an individual is evaluated without a solver call, and the search therefore explores thousands of candidate geometries rather than a handful of DSMC cases. The initial population is a Latin hypercube sample of the design box, so the first generation is stratified in every coordinate; no factorial screen and no fitted response surface are used.

\section{Research Design and Workflow}
This chapter outlines the computational framework utilized to investigate the aerothermal effects of surface topology on a Hypersonic Inflatable Aerodynamic Decelerator (HIAD). The research workflow follows a multi-stage numerical analysis approach, moving from parametric geometry generation to high-fidelity Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) simulation and Genetic Algorithm (GA) optimization.

To systematically isolate the effects of the ``scalloped'' surface geometry, the methodology is designed to compare a baseline smooth sphere-cone against the proposed inflatable architecture under identical hypersonic flight conditions. The process is divided into five primary stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parametric Geometry Generation:} Automated 3D CAD generation of the scalloped (inflatable) and smooth (baseline) profiles using CadQuery Python scripting, enabling parametric variation of toroid count, cone angle, nose radius, and aeroshell diameter.
    \item \textbf{DSMC Simulation Setup:} Configuration of the SPARTA solver with freestream conditions matching the target trajectory point, including the 5-species air model (N$_2$, O$_2$, NO, N, O) with Variable Soft Sphere (VSS) collision physics.
    \item \textbf{Particle-Based Simulation:} Execution of the DSMC simulation within a Docker-containerized Linux environment, tracking millions of simulated particles through advection, molecular collisions, and surface interactions.

    \item \textbf{Survivability Optimization:} Simple Genetic Algorithm (GA) with tournament selection, uniform crossover, Gaussian mutation and elitism for automated multi-objective optimization of HIAD geometry.
    \item \textbf{Validation and Analysis:} Validation of the solution through a four-level grid independence study ($70\times70$, $100\times100$, $139\times139$ and $200\times200$ cells, Figure~\ref{fig:grid_independence}) and validation of thermal results against the Sutton-Graves stagnation-point correlation and IRVE-3 flight data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{DSMC Simulation Framework}

\subsection{Governing Equations: The Boltzmann Equation}
Unlike continuum CFD methods that solve the Reynolds-Averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) equations, this study employs the Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method, which solves the Boltzmann equation for rarefied gas dynamics~\cite{bird1994molecular}. The Boltzmann equation describes the evolution of the molecular velocity distribution function $f(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{v}, t)$:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{r}} f + \frac{\mathbf{F}}{m} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{v}} f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)_{coll}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{F}$ is the external force per molecule, $m$ is the molecular mass, and the right-hand side represents the Boltzmann collision integral. DSMC provides a stochastic solution to this equation by tracking representative particles through advection and collisions.

\subsection{Knudsen Number and Flow Regime}
The validity of the continuum assumption is governed by the Knudsen number ($Kn$), defined as the ratio of the molecular mean free path ($\lambda$) to the characteristic length ($L$):

\begin{equation}
    Kn = \frac{\lambda}{L}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n}
\end{equation}

where $d$ is the molecular diameter and $n$ is the number density. For HIAD reentry at altitudes of 50--60\,km, the flow is in the transitional regime ($0.01 < Kn < 1$), where continuum CFD methods (RANS/LES) are physically inappropriate and DSMC is required~\cite{bird1994molecular}.

\subsection{SPARTA Solver Configuration}
The simulations are performed using the SPARTA (Sandia Parallel Algorithm for Real-Time Application) DSMC solver~\cite{plimpton2014sparta}. SPARTA is executed within a Docker-containerized Linux environment to ensure reproducibility of the build and execution across platforms.

\subsubsection*{Freestream Conditions}
The freestream conditions are prescribed to match the target trajectory point for IRVE-3 reentry at 51.8\,km altitude. All atmospheric values below are obtained from the atmospheric model evaluated in the solver at that altitude, not from an independent hand calculation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Freestream Velocity ($v_{\infty}$):} 3378\,m/s (corresponding to Mach $\approx$ 10.29 at altitude)
    \item \textbf{Number Density ($n$):} $1.63 \times 10^{22}$\,m$^{-3}$ (derived from atmospheric density $\rho = 7.851 \times 10^{-4}$\,kg/m$^3$ via $n = \rho N_A / M_{air}$)
    \item \textbf{Freestream Temperature ($T_{\infty}$):} 268.41\,K (atmospheric model, code baseline)
    \item \textbf{Particle Weighting Factor ($f_{num}$):} $1.5 \times 10^{20}$ (configurable via \texttt{--fnum})
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Gas Model}
A realistic 5-species air model is employed: N$_2$, O$_2$, NO, N, and O. The collision physics use the Variable Soft Sphere (VSS) model, where the collision cross-section varies with relative velocity:

\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \pi d_{ref}^2 \left( \frac{g_{ref}}{g} \right)^{2(\omega - 0.5)}
\end{equation}

where $g$ is the relative velocity of colliding molecules and $\omega$ is the viscosity index specific to each gas species. The VSS model captures the molecular-level interactions including vibrational energy exchange and dissociation reactions relevant to hypersonic entry conditions. The wall interaction is fully diffuse with an accommodation coefficient of $1.0$ (\texttt{surf\_collide 1 diffuse 1.0} in the solver input), so all incident molecular energy is transferred to the surface at the wall temperature; this is the standard fully-accommodated (thermal equilibrium) assumption for a cold wall.

\subsubsection*{Particle Tracking}
SPARTA tracks millions of simulated particles through three fundamental processes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Advection:} Particles are moved ballistically through the domain according to their velocity vectors.
    \item \textbf{Collisions:} Molecular collisions are modeled using the VSS collision model within computational cells, with collision partners selected using the nearest-neighbor pairing scheme.
    \item \textbf{Surface Interactions:} Particles hitting the vehicle surface are reflected according to the specified gas-surface interaction model (diffuse or specular reflection).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cartesian Grid and Collision Pairing}
While DSMC is a particle method, a Cartesian computational grid is required for two purposes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Collision Pairing:} Restricting collision partner selection to particles within the same cell ensures $O(N)$ computational complexity rather than $O(N^2)$ for brute-force pairing.
    \item \textbf{Macroscopic Sampling:} Cells serve as sampling volumes for accumulating density, temperature, pressure, and velocity statistics from the particle data.
\end{enumerate}

The grid resolution must be finer than the local mean free path ($\lambda$) to resolve the shock structure and boundary layer gradients. A grid-factor parameter (default: 0.7) controls the mesh density multiplier; the grid independence of that choice was verified by a four-level study ($70\times70$, $100\times100$, $139\times139$ and $200\times200$ cells, Figure~\ref{fig:grid_independence} and Table~\ref{tab:grid_study}).

\subsection{Ballistic Coefficient (\texorpdfstring{$\beta$}{beta})}
\label{sec:ballistic}
The ballistic coefficient is a measure of the vehicle's ability to maintain its speed during reentry~\cite{AndersonHypersonic}:

\begin{equation}
    \beta = \frac{m}{C_D A}
\end{equation}

Since $F_{drag} = C_D A q$, where $q$ is dynamic pressure:

\begin{equation}
    \beta = \frac{m \cdot q}{F_{drag}}
\end{equation}

The mass density is derived from number density:

\begin{equation}
    \rho = n \cdot \frac{M_{air}}{N_A}
\end{equation}

where $M_{air} \approx 28.97$\,g/mol and $N_A$ is Avogadro's constant. The dynamic pressure is:

\begin{equation}
    q = \frac{1}{2} \rho v_{\infty}^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{Instantaneous Deceleration (\texorpdfstring{$n$}{n})}
The deceleration load felt by the vehicle in Earth-gravity units ($g_0$):

\begin{equation}
    n = \frac{F_{drag}}{m \cdot g_0}
\end{equation}

\section{Aerothermodynamic Analysis}

\subsection{Stagnation Heat Flux -- Sutton-Graves Correlation}
The Sutton-Graves (1971) stagnation-point convective heating correlation is used for all absolute thermal calculations~\cite{sutton1971gravesh}. This is the standard reference formula for Earth entry and has been validated against IRVE-3 flight data by Rapisarda (2023)~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao}:

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\dot{q}_{stag} = C_{SG} \sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\infty}}{R_N}} \cdot V_{\infty}^3}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_{SG} = 1.7415 \times 10^{-4}$ (Sutton--Graves constant for Earth air)
    \item $\rho_{\infty}$: Freestream density (kg/m$^3$)
    \item $R_N$: Nose radius (m)
    \item $V_{\infty}$: Freestream velocity (m/s)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{IRVE-3 Validation}
For the IRVE-3 baseline configuration the correlation is evaluated at the SPARTA DSMC run condition --- $h = 51.8$\,km under ISA, which yields $\rho_{\infty} = 7.851 \times 10^{-4}$\,kg/m$^3$ --- using the IRVE-3 sphere--cone nose radius $R_N = 1.5$\,m (a 3\,m aeroshell diameter) and the run velocity $V_{\infty} = 3{,}378$\,m/s:

\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{stag} = 1.7415 \times 10^{-4} \sqrt{\frac{7.851 \times 10^{-4}}{1.5}} \times 3378^3 \approx 1.536 \times 10^{5} \ \text{W/m}^2 = 15.36 \ \text{W/cm}^2
\end{equation}

The correlation constant is the value implemented in the Ada/SPARK model, $C_{SG} = 1.7415 \times 10^{-4}$; an earlier revision of this work used $1.83 \times 10^{-4}$, which raises the computed flux by about $5\,\%$ and produced the $16.14$\,W/cm$^2$ quoted in that revision. The evaluation above uses the $1.5$\,m body radius as the nose radius, which is the convention of the validation comparison. The optimizer evaluates the same correlation at the physical nose radius $R_n=\kappa X_1$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:nose_ratio} ($0.55$\,m at the baseline), which is why its screening flux is $25.36$\,W/cm$^2$ at the baseline. The $12.20$\,W/cm$^2$ value in the validation export is a third, legacy evaluation at a hardcoded $V_{\infty} = 2700$\,m/s and $\rho_{\infty} = 6.9674 \times 10^{-4}$\,kg/m$^3$; it is not the run condition and is not used here. Rapisarda (2023) reports a trajectory-integrated Sutton-Graves peak of 15.26\,W/cm$^2$ (+6.26\% above the 14.36\,W/cm$^2$ flight measurement) and a Fay--Riddell CFD prediction of 13.83\,W/cm$^2$ (-3.69\% below flight)~\cite{rapisarda2023mdao}. The Fay--Riddell correlation is more physically accurate for HIAD geometries because it accounts for the actual shock layer structure.

\subsubsection*{Note on SPARTA Surface Energy}
The raw SPARTA DSMC kinetic energy ($ke$) surface compute cannot be used directly as an absolute heat flux in W/m$^2$. SPARTA's \texttt{fix ave/surf} outputs kinetic energy per simulated particle per timestep per face element -- a simulation-internal unit that depends on $f_{num}$, timestep $dt$, and face area in a non-trivially-normalized way. It is retained only as a \textbf{relative shape-ranking signal} for the optimizer (comparing geometries against each other). For all absolute thermal calculations, the Sutton-Graves correlation is used.

\subsection{Surface Temperature -- Radiative Equilibrium}
At the TPS outer surface, radiative cooling balances the incoming convective heat flux at steady state. Assuming grey-body radiation:

\begin{equation}
    T_{surface} = \left(\frac{\dot{q}_{stag}}{\epsilon \sigma}\right)^{1/4}
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon = 0.75$ (Nicalon SiC emissivity) and $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$\,W/m$^2$K$^4$ (Stefan-Boltzmann constant).

\subsection{Backface Temperature -- 1D Transient Model}
The 1D transient backface temperature model estimates the thermal penetration through the FTPS insulation stack:

\begin{equation}
    T_{back} = T_{init} + \frac{\dot{q} \cdot \Delta t \cdot \eta_{lag}}{\rho_{TPS} \cdot C_{p,TPS} \cdot \delta_{TPS}}
\end{equation}

where $\eta_{lag} = 0.15$ is the thermal lag factor, $\rho_{TPS} = 1468$\,kg/m$^3$ (Nicalon SiC density), $C_{p,TPS} = 1100$\,J/kg$\cdot$K, and $\delta_{TPS}$ is the TPS thickness.

\subsection{Composite Thermal-Propagation Chain}
\label{sec:thermal_chain}

The two lumped models above are the legacy interface of the thermal model: a radiative-equilibrium surface temperature and a single-slab backface temperature. The framework implements the full propagation chain behind them, per surface element, as a layered one-dimensional solver: the convective load enters the outer ply, is conducted inwards through the layup, is re-radiated from the hot face, and the resulting in-plane coupling is closed with a lumped lateral term.

The hot-face boundary condition balances the two outward paths, conduction through the layup resistance $R=\sum_i \delta_i/k_i$ and radiation to the freestream,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{wall} = \frac{T_{surf}-T_{back}}{R} + \epsilon\sigma\left(T_{surf}^{4}-T_{\infty}^{4}\right),
    \label{eq:thermal_balance}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$ are as above and $T_{back}$ is supplied by the transient model of this section. The left-hand side is monotone in $T_{surf}$, so the equation has a unique root, which the solver finds by bisection and Newton iteration; the solver is registered in the unit-test suite, so the root exists and is unique for every element.

The lateral (in-plane) term is applied \emph{after} the one-dimensional solve and is not a two-dimensional PDE: each element exchanges heat with its neighbours through a lumped conductance $G_{lat}$,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{lateral} = G_{lat}\left(T_{surf}-T_{surf}^{neighbours}\right),
    \label{eq:lateral}
\end{equation}
and the resulting source is added to the element's energy balance. The measured magnitude is negligible for this geometry: for the reference run the first element carries $\dot{q}_{conv}=143{,}760$\,W/m$^2$, $\dot{q}_{cond}=3{,}362$\,W/m$^2$, $\dot{q}_{rad}=140{,}399$\,W/m$^2$ and $\dot{q}_{lateral}=0.0083$\,W/m$^2$, i.e.\ about $6\times10^{-8}$ of the convective load, with $T_{surf}=1327.2$\,K, $T_{back}=300$\,K and $R=0.3056$\,m$^2$K/W across the 3\,648 surface elements and the five-ply layup.

The per-element results are exported to the run's \texttt{results/tps\_conduction} tree (element table, per-ply table, through-thickness profiles and a summary), and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports them.


\subsection{Statistical Convergence Monitoring}
As a Monte Carlo method, DSMC produces statistical noise that scales as $1/\sqrt{N_{\text{samples}}}$, where $N_{\text{samples}}$ is the number of independent particle-time samples accumulated. Convergence is monitored by tracking the running average of surface force and energy quantities. SPARTA's \texttt{\seqsplit{stats\_interval}} parameter (default: 100 steps) controls the frequency of convergence diagnostics. The simulation is considered statistically converged when the relative change in drag coefficient ($C_d$) and stagnation heat flux falls below 2\% over consecutive monitoring windows. For the baseline optimization runs, convergence is typically achieved after 500--1100 particle-time steps depending on the geometry complexity.

\subsection{Design Space and Parameters}
The search acts on the effective design vector $\mathbf{x}=(X_1,X_2,X_3,N_t,m)$: the body radius $X_1=D/2$, the torus minor radius $X_2=r_{tor}$, the half-cone angle $X_3=\theta_c$, the toroid count $N_t$ and the vehicle mass $m$. The bounds are the defaults of the production runner (Table~\ref{tab:design_variables}). The mass cancels in the ballistic-coefficient ratio and therefore does not change the geometry that the cost sees; it is carried because it determines the physical vehicle and the deceleration load, and the toroid count is fixed at six in the recorded campaign. The nose bluntness used by the Sutton--Graves correlation is not an independent coordinate: it is derived from the body radius as
\begin{equation}
    R_n = \kappa X_1, \qquad \kappa = 0.3667,
    \label{eq:nose_ratio}
\end{equation}
which maps the $1.5$\,m reference body radius onto the $0.55$\,m nose flown by the reference DSMC run.

A design is admissible only inside the structural envelope. The maximum body radius derived from $(X_1,X_2,X_3,N_t)$ must not exceed $R_{\max} = 3.0$\,m (the IRVE-3 diameter limit), the body radius must not fall below $1.0$\,m, and the nose cap must house the payload cavity. These limits enter the cost function as quadratic penalties rather than as hard filters, so the population is never blocked from moving through an infeasible region on its way to a feasible one.

\subsection{Latin Hypercube Initialisation}
The initial population is a Latin hypercube (LHS) over the five effective coordinates~\cite{mckay1979lhs}. Each coordinate is divided into $P=50$ equal strata, the stratum assignment is a fresh random permutation for every coordinate, and one individual is placed inside each stratum of each coordinate. The design is therefore stratified in every one-dimensional marginal: no stratum is empty and no stratum receives two individuals, which is the property that a uniform random draw does not have. The stratified start covers the box before the first fitness evaluation and avoids the clustering that otherwise leaves whole regions of the search space unvisited.

The LHS is the only seeding the GA uses. There are no factorial points, no axial points and no Bayesian seed: the same fifty individuals are both the initial sample and the first generation, and each of them is evaluated with the analytic cost. The generator that draws the stratum permutations and the mutation offsets is seeded with the fixed value $42$, so the campaign is reproducible: two runs of the same binary on the same inputs return the same generation history and the same optimum. A Latin hypercube is also the natural choice here because the objective is cheap: the reason to use a space-filling design is coverage, not the construction of a surrogate.

\subsection{Cost Function}
The cost is a weighted sum of four normalized physical terms plus a constraint penalty, all evaluated in closed form from the geometry and the validated flight condition. The frontal geometry is the outermost edge of the toroid stack,
\begin{equation}
    R_{\max} = X_1\cos\gamma + (2N_t-1)X_2\cos\gamma + X_2,
    \qquad \gamma = \frac{\pi}{2} - X_3,
    \label{eq:rmax}
\end{equation}
so that the reference area is $A=\pi R_{\max}^2$. The stagnation heat flux is the Sutton--Graves correlation evaluated at the physical nose radius of Eq.~\eqref{eq:nose_ratio},
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{SG} = C_{SG}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_\infty}{R_n}}\,V_\infty^3,
    \qquad C_{SG}=1.7415\times10^{-4},
    \label{eq:sutton_graves}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_\infty=7.851\times10^{-4}$\,kg/m\textsuperscript{3} and $V_\infty=3{,}378$\,m/s at the validated snapshot. The heat load is the flux held over the effective heating time,
\begin{equation}
    Q = \dot{q}_{SG}\,\tau, \qquad \tau = 13.582459\,\text{s}.
    \label{eq:heat_load}
\end{equation}
The ballistic coefficient is evaluated as a ratio in which the mass cancels,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\beta}{\beta_{ref}} = \frac{C_{d,ref}\,A_{ref}}{C_d\,A},
    \label{eq:beta_ratio}
\end{equation}
and the drag coefficient follows the geometric law used by the model,
\begin{equation}
    C_d = C_{d,0}\left(\frac{R_{\max}}{R_{ref}}\right)^{0.15}
          \left[1+0.05\left(\frac{R_{ref}}{X_1}-1\right)\right],
    \label{eq:cd_law}
\end{equation}
with $C_{d,0}=1.47$ and $R_{ref}=1.5$\,m. Equations~\eqref{eq:rmax}--\eqref{eq:cd_law} are the complete analytic model used to score every LHS and GA individual; no solver call is made inside the loop.

The cost itself is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    J(\mathbf{x}) = {} & W_{HL}\frac{Q(\mathbf{x})}{Q_{target}} + W_{q}\frac{\dot{q}_{SG}(\mathbf{x})}{\dot{q}_{ref}} + W_{\beta}\left(\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\beta_{ref}} - 1\right)^{2} \\
    & + W_{Cd}\max\!\left(0, \frac{C_{d,goal} - C_d(\mathbf{x})}{C_{d,goal}}\right) + \Lambda_{pen}\,\Phi(\mathbf{x}),
\end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ga_cost}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Q(\mathbf{x})$ is the heat load of Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat_load}, $\dot{q}_{SG}(\mathbf{x})$ is the stagnation flux of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sutton_graves}, $\beta(\mathbf{x})$ is the ballistic coefficient of Eq.~\eqref{eq:beta_ratio}, and $C_d(\mathbf{x})$ is the drag coefficient of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cd_law}. The reference values $\beta_{ref}$ and $C_{d,ref}$ are the validated IRVE-3-adapted baseline, and $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ collects the envelope and payload penalties:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\mathbf{x}) = \max(0, R_{\max}(\mathbf{x}) - 3.0)^{2} + \max(0, 1.0 - X_1)^{2} + \max(0, R_{N,payload} - X_1)^{2},
    \label{eq:penalty}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $R_{N,payload}=\sqrt{r_{pay}^2+(h_{pay}/2)^2}+s = 1.0434$\,m the body radius required to house the cylindrical payload ($r_{pay}=0.275$\,m, $h_{pay}=1.7$\,m, standoff $s=0.15$\,m) inside the nose cap. The weights are fixed at $W_{HL} = 1.0$, $W_{q} = 0.5$, $W_{\beta} = 0.5$, $W_{Cd} = 30$ and $\Lambda_{pen} = 100$. The heat-load term carries the largest physical weight, but the drag term is a normalized deficit and is deliberately weighted high: with $C_{d,goal} = 1.70$, above the largest drag reachable inside the penalty-free envelope, every attainable increase in drag is rewarded. The chosen weight therefore selects the drag regime of the heat-load/drag Pareto front, a trade-off that is reported explicitly in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} rather than hidden inside the objective.

All terms are dimensionless and non-negative, so $J(\mathbf{x}) \ge 0$ by construction, and the cost is analytic: its evaluation requires no solver call. The high-fidelity DSMC solution enters only through the validation of the closed-form correlations, not through the optimization loop.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm}
\label{sec:ga_operators}

The GA maintains a population of $P = 50$ designs and evolves it for at most $G_{\max} = 200$ generations. The implementation uses the following operators~\cite{Goldberg1989}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initialisation.} The first generation is the Latin hypercube of Sec.~\ref{sec:ga_opt}: one individual per stratum of every coordinate, with the stratum assignment drawn independently for each coordinate. There is no uniform-random start, no factorial design and no surrogate-trained start.

    \item \textbf{Selection.} Tournament selection with tournament size $k_t = 3$. Three individuals are drawn at random and the one with the lowest cost becomes a parent. Tournament selection needs no fitness scaling and cannot be dominated by a single super-individual, which keeps selection pressure moderate while the population is still dispersed.

    \item \textbf{Crossover.} Uniform crossover with probability $p_c = 0.7$. For each child, every gene is inherited from either parent according to an independent Bernoulli mask; if crossover is not applied, the child is a copy of one parent. Uniform crossover treats the coordinates symmetrically, which matters because the body radius and the torus radius act on different terms of the cost.

    \item \textbf{Mutation.} Gaussian mutation applied gene by gene with probability $p_m = 0.1$. A mutated gene is perturbed by a zero-mean Gaussian whose standard deviation is 10\,\% of that gene's range in Table~\ref{tab:design_variables}, and the result is clamped to the search box. Mutation is the only operator that can introduce a value absent from the current population.

    \item \textbf{Elitism.} The best $E = 2$ individuals are copied unchanged into the next generation. Elitism guarantees that the recorded best cost is non-increasing, which is what makes the convergence history of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} readable as a monotone curve.
\end{enumerate}

The population is evaluated concurrently. A generation is embarrassingly parallel because each individual's cost depends only on that individual, so a fixed pool of worker tasks claims indices from the published generation and stores one cost per index; the master waits for the whole batch before breeding. The worker pool applies the Ravenscar restriction set explicitly in its context clause, which bounds the tasking to a static set of tasks with protected-object rendezvous and no heap allocation. The parallel result is identical to the sequential result by construction: the evaluation of an index depends on that index alone, so the order in which results are stored cannot change any cost.

The search terminates when the generation cap is reached or when the best cost has not improved by more than $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ for $G_{pat} = 20$ consecutive generations. The stagnation counter is maintained by a SPARK-proved core routine that saturates instead of overflowing and rejects non-finite costs, so a failed evaluation is counted as ``not stagnating'' and the search continues rather than silently spinning. The recorded campaign reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} converged on the stagnation criterion after 38 generations and 1900 individual evaluations; the corrected pass-2 re-run used 201 generations and 10050 evaluations.

\subsection{Double Pass GA Optimization}
\label{sec:double_pass_ga}

The optimization is organized as two passes of the same genetic algorithm, separated by high-fidelity re-simulation. The separation is deliberate. The closed-form cost is cheap enough to be evaluated thousands of times, so it is used for the search itself; the quantity it is supposed to predict --- the heat load of the vehicle --- is expensive, so it is measured only where a decision is taken.

\textbf{Pass 1 (model space).} The GA of Sec.~\ref{sec:ga_operators} minimizes the cost of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ga_cost} over the design box, every individual evaluated in closed form. No solver call is made inside this pass: the search explores thousands of candidate geometries, and the winner $\mathbf{x}^{(1)}$ is the optimum of the stated analytic objective.

\textbf{Validation of the pass-1 winner (physical space).} $\mathbf{x}^{(1)}$ is converted to a vehicle with Eq.~\eqref{eq:nose_ratio} and re-simulated with the full DSMC chain at 2200 steps; the refined pipeline returns the measured heat load $q_{hybrid}$ of that geometry. This is the only high-fidelity run of pass 1: the solver measures one design, it does not search.

\textbf{The correction factor.} The factor is a fidelity ratio between the high-fidelity solver and the correlation it is supposed to reproduce, and it is bounded before use,
\begin{equation}
    c = \operatorname{clamp}\Bigl(\frac{q^{\mathrm{DSMC}}_{\mathrm{flux}}}{q^{\mathrm{SG}}_{\mathrm{flux}}},\;\allowbreak 0.5,\;\allowbreak 2.0\Bigr),
    \label{eq:correction_factor}
\end{equation}
where the numerator is the peak surface convection flux measured by the DSMC run and the denominator is the Sutton--Graves prediction for the \emph{same} flown nose radius at the \emph{same} flight state, both read from the same row of the same run. The factor is therefore dimensionless, and it is bounded so that a single pathological simulation can neither collapse nor invert the objective.

Two properties of this construction are deliberate. First, the ratio is taken \emph{within} one run, not between the run and the screening model: the screening model evaluates its heat flux at the peak-heating snapshot of the trajectory ($51.8$\,km, $3379$\,m/s) while the run records its own correlation column at the baseline flight state ($2700$\,m/s), so a ratio taken across the two would measure the difference between two flight states rather than the fidelity of the correlation, and it would be pinned at the lower clamp for every geometry. Second, the factor is applied to the heat-load term alone: the drag and ballistic-coefficient terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ga_cost} are geometric integrals rather than thermal correlations, so they carry no thermal-model bias and are left untouched; correcting them would amount to fitting the geometry instead of the physics.

\textbf{Pass 2 (model space).} The corrected objective is
\begin{equation}
    J^{(2)}(\mathbf{x}) = J(\mathbf{x}) + (c-1)\,\frac{Q(\mathbf{x})}{Q_{target}},
    \label{eq:corrected_cost}
\end{equation}
which, with the unit weight of the heat-load term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ga_cost}, is algebraically identical to re-weighting that term by $c$: the second pass therefore penalises the heat load of a design by the factor by which the solver has shown that the correlation under-states it. The implementation therefore injects the correction as an effective heat-load weight and re-runs the \emph{same} genetic algorithm, with the same population, operators and bounds: there is no second cost function and no surrogate of the corrected objective. Pass 2 is again a purely analytic pass, and its winner $\mathbf{x}^{(2)}$ is the corrected design.

\textbf{Validation of the pass-2 winner (physical space).} $\mathbf{x}^{(2)}$ is re-simulated with the same 2200-step DSMC protocol. The two validations --- one per pass --- are the only high-fidelity runs of the pipeline. The bounded factor makes the correction conservative: because $|c-1| \leq 1$, the corrected cost can at most halve or double the weight of the heat-load term, so the second pass refines the design against the measured bias of the model without being able to run away from it.

\section{Boundary Conditions and Domain Setup}

\subsection{Freestream Inlet}
The simulation domain is initialized with freestream particles injected from the inlet boundary. The particle velocity distribution follows the Maxwell--Boltzmann distribution at the prescribed freestream temperature and velocity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Velocity:} Directed flow at $v_{\infty} = 3378$\,m/s
    \item \textbf{Temperature:} $T_{\infty} = 268.41$\,K
    \item \textbf{Species Fractions:} N$_2$ (78\%), O$_2$ (21\%), NO (1\%) at freestream; dissociated species (N, O) generated through collisions in the shock layer
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Vehicle Surface}
The vehicle surface is modeled as a diffuse reflection boundary with fully accommodate thermal wall conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wall Temperature:} $T_{wall} = 1000$\,K (isothermal), matching the \texttt{surf\_collide 1 diffuse 1000 1.0} line of the archived SPARTA input \texttt{in.hiad}
    \item \textbf{Reflection Model:} Diffuse (maximally entropic)
    \item \textbf{Catalytic Efficiency:} Not specified in the solver input (default non-catalytic wall); gas-phase reactions are enabled via \texttt{react tce air.react}, and no \texttt{surf\_react} command is issued, so dissociated species recombine only in the gas phase.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Symmetry and Outflow}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Symmetry Plane:} For 2D axisymmetric simulations, the centerline enforces rotational symmetry.
    \item \textbf{Outflow:} Particles leaving the domain are deleted and re-injected at the inlet to maintain steady-state mass flux.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Knudsen Number Field}
The local Knudsen number is computed from the SPARTA grid data:

\begin{equation}
    Kn_{local} = \frac{\lambda_{local}}{L} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n_{local} L}
\end{equation}

This enables the identification of continuum ($Kn < 0.01$), transitional ($0.01 < Kn < 1$), and, where applicable, free-molecular ($Kn > 1$) regions within the flow field.

Two distinct tolerance statements apply to this table. First, the MDAO reference model reproduces the flight heat flux and heat load to within $-3.7\%$ and $+0.06\%$ respectively, comfortably inside the $\pm 10\%$ margin normally required of a calibrated reconstruction; this confirms that the IRVE-3 flight data adopted here are internally consistent and suitable as a validation target. Second, the geometry-sensitive quantities computed by this work satisfy that same margin: the ballistic coefficient deviates by $+3.0\%$ and the drag coefficient is 1.4625.

The single-trajectory-point evaluation of the DSMC solution does \emph{not} satisfy the $\pm 10\%$ margin, but the deviations fall in opposite directions on the two classes of quantity and have different causes. The two \emph{integrated} quantities are under-predicted for the heat load and over-predicted for the deceleration: total heat load by $-15.0\%$ and peak deceleration by $+15.3\%$. The flight reconstruction and the MDAO model integrate the heating and deceleration history across the complete reentry, whereas the DSMC run samples a single trajectory point ($h = 51.8$\,km, Mach 10.29, $V = 3{,}378$\,m/s) under ISA conditions; integrating over the full trajectory raises the accumulated heat load towards the measured value, while the deceleration peak occurs in the early transient at the high-dynamic-pressure condition, which is a different instant from the flight peak. The \emph{pointwise} stagnation heat flux behaves oppositely, over-predicting by $+6.9\%$, because the Sutton--Graves figure is a continuum correlation built on a spherical nose cap: it carries neither the heat-shielding effect of the scalloped torus nor the rarefied-flow departure that the DSMC solver resolves, and it therefore acts as a conservative upper bound on the stagnation flux at this condition. The ballistic coefficient closes the argument from the other side, agreeing to $+3.0\%$ and confirming that the scalloped geometry is reproduced correctly. The numerical framework is consequently judged sound on the strength of the geometry-sensitive agreement, with the trajectory-integration gap and the correlation-versus-solver gap carried forward separately and explicitly as stated limitations rather than absorbed into a tolerance claim.

\section{Computational Infrastructure}

\subsection{Docker-Containerized Execution}
SPARTA simulations are executed within a Docker-containerized Linux environment to ensure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reproducibility of the SPARTA build across platforms
    \item Isolation from host OS dependencies
    \item Consistent library versions and compiler toolchains
\end{itemize}

The Python optimization environment runs on the native OS; the analytic cost of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ga_cost} is evaluated in closed form, so the search itself requires no GPU acceleration.

\section{Computational and Optimization Standards}
\label{sec:comp_standards}

This section records the standards that make the optimization campaign reproducible and auditable. The framework has no trained machine-learning surrogate in its main path: the cost is evaluated analytically and the search is a genetic algorithm, so the reproducibility questions are those of a numerical optimization campaign rather than those of a model-training pipeline.

\subsection{Evaluation Pipeline}
A candidate design is converted into a geometry, and the cost of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ga_cost} is evaluated in closed form with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:rmax}--\eqref{eq:cd_law}: the Sutton--Graves correlation gives the stagnation heat flux at the physical nose radius, the flux is held over the effective heating time to give the total heat load, and the ballistic coefficient and drag coefficient follow from the geometry. No solver call is made inside the optimization loop. The DSMC solution is used to validate the closed-form correlations against flight data (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}), not to evaluate individuals, which is why a population of 50 can be evaluated for hundreds of generations at negligible cost.

The optimizer writes a single JSON artifact containing the run configuration, the per-generation best history, and the default and optimized metrics.
